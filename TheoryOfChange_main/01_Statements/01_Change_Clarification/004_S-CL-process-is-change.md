# If process means nothing over and above happening, the dispute is verbal

## Claim (formal)
If a process ontology uses “process” to mean nothing over and above happening, unfolding, or becoming, then the disagreement with CO is not substantive at the primitive level.

## Philosophical Translation (of formal claim)
CO does not need an artificial fight with positions that mean the same primitive under another label. If “process” simply names occurring or happening itself, then the core dispute is largely terminological.

## Philosophical Justification
A good comparative critique must avoid strawmen. Some process philosophers may genuinely be pointing at the same primitive pressure that CO wants to preserve: the priority of happening over static substance. In those cases, philosophical honesty requires admitting that the difference may be largely lexical or one of emphasis.

This file therefore performs a charitable narrowing move: not every use of “process” is an opponent. Some are near-equivalent formulations.

## Explanation (informal)
This file is here to prevent fake victories. If another view already places happening at the root and does not add more conceptual weight than that, then CO should not attack it as if it were a different ontology simply because the label differs.

## Derivation (Philosophical)
- CO wants the primitive to be happening/change.
- A rival may use the label “process.”
- If the rival means only what CO means by happening, then there is no deeper disagreement at that level.
- Therefore the dispute is verbal rather than substantive.

## Clarifications / Further Context
- This file does **not** say all process ontologies are equivalent to CO.
- It says only that some may be.
- The real question is not the word but the conceptual load carried by the word.

## Objections addressed

### Objection 1: “Why keep the CO label at all, then?”
Because words guide attention. CO’s claim is that “change” or “happening” often better protects the primitive against thicker presuppositions than “process” does. But where the concepts coincide, the disagreement is not fundamental.

## What this file establishes
This file establishes a rule of fair comparison:
- near-equivalent rival concepts should be treated as near-equivalent.

## What this file does not yet establish
It does **not** yet tell us when process is thicker than happening. That is the task of the next file.
